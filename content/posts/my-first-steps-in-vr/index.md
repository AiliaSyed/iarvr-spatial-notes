## When I first got into VR

My introduction to virtual reality was through the **Oculus Rift S**, which I used for quite a long time. There were already games that made the technology feel special: *Beat Saber* quickly became a classic for me, and puzzle games such as *I Expect You to Die* and *The Room VR* showed how well immersive interaction could work.

The experiences themselves were fun. But something kept getting in the way of enjoying them: everything around actually starting a VR session.

## The cable was always part of the experience

The thing that bothered me most about the Rift S was the **cable connecting the headset to the PC**. It limited where I could move, and I constantly had to think about whether there was enough space around me.

Before playing, I often needed to clear an area, get the computer ready, and check that the cable could even reach the free space. Sometimes the PC was simply too far away from the spot where I wanted to play.

That may sound like a small inconvenience, but it influenced how often I felt like putting on the headset. Even a good game can be less inviting when starting it feels like a project of its own.

## When technical interruptions break immersion

My experience also depended heavily on the PC. Although the Rift S was advertised as accessible with relatively modest PC hardware, I personally encountered freezes, crashes, and games that sometimes did not load correctly.

These interruptions felt especially frustrating in VR. When an application froze or the headset stopped working as expected, I was pulled straight out of the virtual environment.

To me, that is one of the clearest examples of how **technical reliability affects immersion**. The virtual world might be convincing, but it becomes much harder to stay engaged when I am constantly worrying about the setup.

I still enjoyed games like *Beat Saber*, *I Expect You to Die* and *The Room VR*. I just did not always enjoy the process required to get there.

## Trying the Quest 3S: a different kind of convenience

When I tried the **Meta Quest 3S**, I immediately noticed a difference in how approachable VR felt.

For the games and experiences I tried, performance felt smoother and the overall experience seemed more stable. I also preferred the visual experience, although this is a personal impression rather than a technical comparison of display specifications.

The biggest change, however, was the **standalone design**.

I could put on the headset and play without first turning on my PC, setting everything up, finding enough cable length, or troubleshooting a PC connection. Suddenly, playing a few rounds of *Beat Saber* in between other activities felt natural.

This made VR feel less like something I had to prepare for and more like something I could simply do.

## Controllers and tracking

I also felt that controller tracking was smoother and more dependable during my time with the Quest 3S. With my previous setup, losing controller tracking could occasionally interrupt a game. On the newer headset, I felt less worried about handling the controllers in a very particular way just to avoid tracking issues.

That does not mean tracking is perfect, but the difference in my everyday experience was noticeable.

## Hand tracking: exciting, but not flawless

One of the most fascinating features for me was **hand tracking**. I tried it in the Quest's introductory experiences, and being able to interact without holding controllers felt surprisingly natural.

At the same time, I noticed limitations. Some gestures were not recognized immediately, and rotating my hand sometimes caused tracking to disappear.

That contrast is particularly interesting to me: the interaction feels futuristic when it works, but unreliable recognition can quickly make it frustrating.

It made me think about what makes an interaction technique feel intuitive, robust and comfortable — not only in a demonstration, but in repeated everyday use.

## Why this matters for IARVR

These experiences are part of why I am excited about **Interaction in Virtual and Augmented Reality (IARVR)** at TU Darmstadt.

I want to go beyond playing VR games and start understanding how their interactions are designed and implemented. The course gives me the opportunity to work in Unity, experiment with different input methods, and explore interactions I would personally like to use in VR.

It also gives me a reason to pay attention to details I previously noticed only as a player: comfort, tracking robustness, accessibility, and the amount of effort required before an experience can begin.

My next step is **Lab 01: Introduction to Unity**. I will use this journal to document my setup, experiments, difficulties, solutions and reflections as I work through the module.
